# What Mount Sherman Taught Me About Organizational Change

I chose Mount Sherman as my first 14er because it's consistently described as one of the "easiest."

The elevation wasn't what surprised me. It was the footing. Loose rock. Scree. Talus (yeah... I had to look up what that was too when someone mentioned it to me). I spent most of the hike looking down, trying to figure out where to place my next step.

The climb up was manageable...the elevation gain and altitude are no joke, but were expected. The descent, what I anticipated would be the easy part, was mentally exhausting because every step demanded attention. By the time I reached the bottom, my neck hurt from staring at the trail, I was tired, and my ankles were ready to file a complaint.

Then I realized something.

**I barely looked up.**

The scenery was incredible, but I spent most of the hike focused on not losing my footing.

It got me thinking...

## When leaders describe something as "easy," are they describing the outcome or the path?

We do this all the time with organizational change. The new process makes sense. The technology is better. The reorganization is more efficient.

From the summit, it's easy to see the path. From the trail, you're just trying to find your next stable step.

Employees don't experience change from the summit. They experience it one step at a time. Every unclear expectation. Every competing priority. Every new approval. Every time they have to stop and wonder, "Am I doing this right?"

None of those moments seem significant on their own. Together, they determine how confidently people move. Progress slows, energy drains, and from a leadership perspective, it can start to look like resistance.

Sometimes it isn't resistance at all. Sometimes people are simply spending so much energy watching their footing that they can't focus on where they're headed.

Maybe that's one of the most overlooked challenges in organizational change. When people are consumed by figuring out how to navigate the change, they have little capacity left to embrace why the change matters.

One of the biggest lessons I've learned in organizational development is that we spend a lot of time designing the destination. We don't always spend enough time designing the conditions that help people get there.

**The organizations that navigate change best aren't necessarily the ones with the clearest vision of the summit. They're the ones that make the next step feel stable enough for people to keep moving.**
