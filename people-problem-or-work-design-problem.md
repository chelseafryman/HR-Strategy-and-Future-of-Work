# Maybe You Don't Have a People Problem. Maybe You Have a Work Design Problem.

Organizations are often quick to diagnose performance problems as people problems.

- We need stronger talent.  
- Managers need to hold people more accountable.  
- Employees need better skills.  
- We need to hire more people.

Sometimes those things are true. But before adding headcount, replacing people, or launching another training program, I think there's a more fundamental question worth asking:

**Have we designed the work in a way that allows people to be successful?**

Imagine a team that's consistently missing deadlines. The immediate conclusion might be that the team is understaffed or underperforming.

But look closer.

Three different groups have to approve routine decisions. Two teams believe they own the same part of the process. Managers are spending hours reconciling information from systems that don't talk to each other. Employees are doing work that made sense five years ago, but nobody has stopped to ask whether it still needs to be done.

Adding another person might help. It might also add another person to a poorly designed system.

I've seen this firsthand while supporting a business that grew from roughly 130 to more than 200 employees. Growth created an obvious need for talent, but adding people wasn't the whole answer. We also had to look at how the organization was structured, where leadership capability needed to grow, what work belonged where, and whether the roles and processes that worked at 130 employees would still work at 200 and beyond.

*That's one of the things organizational growth has taught me: yesterday's structure can quietly become tomorrow's constraint.*

That's where organizational design and workforce planning need to come together.

Workforce planning can't just ask, "How many people do we need?" It also has to ask: What work actually needs to be done? Where should that work sit? What decisions need to be made, and by whom? What capabilities will the business need next? What work can be simplified, automated, centralized, or eliminated altogether?

Only then does the headcount question become meaningful.

The same is true when performance struggles. If accountability is unclear, decision rights overlap, managers have unreasonable spans of control, or people spend half their day navigating around broken processes, better talent alone won't fix the problem.

You can hire great people into a poorly designed organization and still get mediocre results.

As AI changes how work gets done, I think this distinction becomes even more important. Organizations have an opportunity to redesign work before simply redesigning headcount.

The goal isn't to protect the current structure or automatically reduce it. It's to understand the work the business needs, design the organization around that work, and then determine the workforce required to deliver it.

Redesigning work is often cheaper, faster, and more effective than redesigning headcount.
